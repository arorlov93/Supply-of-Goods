    01

    Specification

    A chemical name is not a specification. ATMP at fifty per cent in water and ATMP at ninety-five per cent as a solid are the same molecule and two different purchases: different freight, different handling, different price per kilogram of the thing that actually works. The first question we ask is never the quantity. It is the active content and the test method it is measured by.

    For a scale inhibitor or a phosphonate, tell us the grade, the active content, the form and the limits that matter to you: chloride, iron, free phosphate, colour. For a coagulant, the alumina content and the basicity, and whether it has to meet a drinking water standard. For an ion exchange resin, the type and matrix, the ionic form it ships in, bead size and uniformity coefficient, moisture retention and total capacity. For a molecular sieve, the type, the form and size, static water adsorption, crush strength and attrition.

    Tell us the end use as well, not only the product. End use decides which impurity matters. A resin for condensate polishing and a resin for metal recovery can carry the same type designation and fail each other's duty.

    02

    Documents first

    We send the technical data sheet, the safety data sheet and a specimen certificate of analysis before any price. They are not formalities and they are not interchangeable. The data sheet is what the producer undertakes to make. The certificate of analysis is what one batch actually was. A producer who will not show a recent certificate before the order is telling you something about the next one.

    The safety data sheet has to be in the format and the language the destination requires, and it carries the facts that decide the shipment: UN number, hazard class, packing group, and the labelling the terminal will look for. Those three lines decide whether a product travels in a standard container, whether the port accepts it, and what the freight costs. A quotation that arrives without them is not yet comparable to anything.

    03

    Quotation

    We quote on one basis: named Incoterm and port, pack and net weight, lead time from order to readiness, the document set that travels with the goods, and how long the price holds. Everything that is not in that line is not in the price.

    Then convert before you compare. Two offers for the same phosphonate, one at fifty per cent active and one at sixty, are not fifteen per cent apart: on active basis the cheaper looking one can be the dearer. Add the pack: drums that are priced with a deposit, bags that the destination will not accept and that have to be repacked, an IBC that cannot be returned. Add the Incoterm: an FOB price and a CIF price are different numbers for the same cargo. We put every offer on the same active basis, the same pack and the same Incoterm, and that comparison is what we send you, with the arithmetic shown.

    04

    Production and inspection

    Production runs against the agreed specification, and every batch leaves with its own certificate of analysis, dated, with the batch number that appears on the drums. Retained samples stay with the producer for the shelf life of the product, which is what makes a later claim possible at all.

    Where the order justifies it, or your tender requires it, an independent inspector attends before shipment. You name the agency, we do not: SGS, Intertek and Bureau Veritas all work at the origins we buy from. The scope is yours to set, and the useful one covers sampling and sealing, quantity and net weight, pack condition and marking, container cleanliness and the seal number. The inspection certificate goes into the document set named in the credit, so the money moves against the goods and not only against paper.

    05

    Delivery

    Pack is a cost decision and it is taken before production, not after. Twenty-five kilogram bags, two hundred and fifty kilogram drums, one tonne intermediate bulk containers and, for liquids in volume, a tank container. Each changes the weight that fits in a container, the labour at the far end and the price per kilogram landed. Wooden pallets travel under the international standard for wood packaging and carry the mark that proves it.

    Temperature is part of the specification, not an afterthought. Several of these products crystallise, stratify or freeze below a stated temperature, and a winter shipment into a continental destination has to say so in the contract. So does shelf life: the date of manufacture on the certificate of analysis, not the date of shipment, is what the clock runs from.

    Goods move by sea, by rail or by road, under our contract from the plant gate to the agreed delivery point, with the documents travelling with them.

    What decides whether a chemical is admissible

    A chemical is admissible or it is not, and that is settled before the order rather than at the border. Three things decide it.

    The first is the regime at destination. Industrial chemicals are registered, notified or licensed under different rules in each market, and the obligation usually sits with the importer rather than with us. We will tell you what the producer can document; what your authority requires of you is yours to confirm, and we would rather raise it in week one than find it at the quay.

    The second is the use. Anything that touches drinking water is judged against a potable water standard, and the certificate has to name the grade and the plant. An industrial grade of the same product, at the same assay, is not a substitute for it and no paperwork makes it one.

    The third is the hazard classification. The UN number, the class and the packing group decide the stowage, the documents, the labelling and often whether a terminal will handle the box at all. They are properties of the product, decided at the plant. A shipment that is right commercially and wrong on one label is still a shipment that does not clear.

    What buyers ask before the first order

    Can you quote a product that is not in the table?

    Usually yes. The table is what we quote most often, not the limit of what we buy. Send the product, the grade and the active content, and we will come back on whether we can source it properly or not. If we cannot, we say so rather than send you a quotation we cannot stand behind.

    What is the smallest order you will handle?

    We work in full container loads, because that is where the economics hold. A first order is normally one or two containers on full commercial terms. Below that the freight per kilogram rises faster than any saving on the goods, and we would be selling you a worse deal than you can get locally.

    Do you supply drinking water grade?

    Where the producer holds the certification for it and can show it by plant and by grade, yes, and the certificate travels with the goods. Where it holds only an industrial grade, we say so and quote the industrial grade as what it is.

    Can we nominate the inspection agency?

    Yes, and we would rather you did. You name the agency and the scope, the inspection happens before shipment, and the certificate goes into the document set named in the credit. An inspector we chose ourselves would prove less to you.

    Can you bid into our tender?

    Yes. For a tender we provide the manufacturer's letter of authorization, certificates of origin and quality and the product documents your committee requires. Send the tender number and the deadline with the enquiry so the file is assembled to the right date.

    How are we protected if the goods are wrong?

    By the structure rather than by a promise. The specification is agreed in writing before production, every batch carries its own certificate of analysis, an inspector you name attends before shipment, and payment is made against the document set named in the credit. Each of those is a point at which a wrong consignment stops instead of arriving.
