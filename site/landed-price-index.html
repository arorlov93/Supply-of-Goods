    01

    The one number most quotations leave out

    Customs authorities publish every import twice: once as the value of the goods at the seller’s door, and once as the value of the same consignment landed at the border. The gap between those two figures is freight and insurance, measured from actual declarations rather than estimated by anybody with something to sell.

    That gap is the line buyers are least able to check and sellers are most likely to leave out. Published as a percentage by origin, it says something uncomfortable and useful: on some goods the country you buy from moves the landed price more than any negotiation on the factory price will.

    The short answer

    1. Freight and insurance ran from 1.2 % to 59.5 % of goods value across the groups below, on United States imports in 2024.
    2. Viet Nam was the heaviest origin and Germany the lightest by median: 10.1 % against 4.0 %.
    3. Cement shows the widest spread: 12.0 % from Poland against 59.5 % from Viet Nam, a gap of 48 points on the same product.

    Source: UN Comtrade reporter data, United States imports, 2024. Method and exclusions below.

    Distance explains less of this than weight does. Power tools out of Viet Nam carry close to nothing, because a tool is worth a great deal per kilogram. Cement out of the same country carries a share many times larger, because cement is worth almost nothing per kilogram and the ship costs the same either way. The rule worth taking away: the cheaper the goods are by weight, the more the origin decides the deal.

    Method, and what this is not

    The figures come from UN Comtrade, which republishes what national customs services report. The reporter here is the United States and the flow is imports, so every row is a declaration made to US customs. Unit values are value divided by net weight. Freight and insurance is the CIF value less the FOB value, as a share of FOB.

    • What is excluded. Rows without a weight or without one of the two values, rows under 50 tonnes for the year, and rows where the uplift came out below one per cent or above sixty. Those last are reporting artefacts rather than freight. 7 rows were dropped on these rules out of 126 collected.
    • What the numbers are not. They are national annual averages across every buyer, every incoterm variant and every box on the water that year. They are a benchmark, not a quotation for your shipment.
    • What they are good for. Telling you whether the freight line in a quotation you are holding is in the right part of the map, and which origins are worth pricing at all before you start.

    Built 2026-10-28 from the 2024 reporting year. We republish it when the source updates. If a figure here looks wrong to you, the source is public and we would rather be corrected than quoted.

    Questions

    Questions about the index

    The four that come up as soon as somebody reads the table.

    Why does freight matter more on some goods than others?

    Because a ship charges for space and weight, not for value. Carrying a tonne of cement costs about what carrying a tonne of power tools costs, but the cement is worth a fraction as much, so the same freight bill is a far larger share of it. That is why the heaviest percentages in the table sit on the cheapest goods.

    Can I use these figures in my own quotation?

    As a sanity check, yes. As a price, no. They are annual national averages across every buyer and every route into one country. If the freight line in a quotation you are holding is far outside the band here, that is worth a question to the seller. If it is inside, it is not proof that the rest of the quotation is fair.

    Why the United States as the reporting country?

    Because it is the market this company sells into, and because its declarations are the ones a buyer here can go and check. The same calculation works for any reporter in the source, and we will publish other corridors as we run them.

    Who can use this table?

    Anyone. It is published under CC BY, the source is named and the method is written out. Cite it, argue with it, or send us a correction.
