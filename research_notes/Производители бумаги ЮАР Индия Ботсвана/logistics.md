# LOGISTICS: Shipping paper reels from India and South Africa to Gaborone, Botswana (research as at 25 September 2026)

**Scope note and honesty warning up front.** This research was conducted on 25 September 2026 using public web sources. Ocean freight rates, THC and trucking rates on this lane are overwhelmingly **quotation-only and not publicly published**. Carrier spot-rate tools (Maersk Spot, MSC, CMA CGM, ONE, Hapag-Lloyd) require login and were not accessible; SeaRates returned HTTP 403. Where a number appears below it is cited and dated. Where no number could be sourced it is recorded as **UNKNOWN / NOT PUBLICLY DISCLOSED** in the Gaps section. **No figure in this document has been invented or back-calculated into a "market rate".** Anything labelled ESTIMATE is explicitly an indicative order-of-magnitude reference point from a third-party guide, **NOT A LIVE QUOTATION**.

**Structural fact that dominates the whole comparison:** Botswana is landlocked and is a member of the Southern African Customs Union (SACU) together with South Africa, Namibia, Lesotho and Eswatini. SACU is a common external tariff area — *"Once goods have cleared customs in one SACU country — at the port of Durban, for instance — they can generally move freely to another member country like Botswana without incurring additional customs duties"* — [Botswana Import Requirements and Documentation](https://www.privacyshield.gov/ps/article?id=Botswana-import-requirements-and-documentation). This is developed in section B3 below, and it is the single biggest lever in the India-vs-South-Africa sourcing decision.

## A. INDIA → BOTSWANA ROUTES (Durban vs Cape Town vs Walvis Bay)

### Takeaway
All three routings are physically possible, but only **India → Durban → road/rail → Gaborone** is supported by dense sailings, a purpose-built rail dry port in Gaborone and a short inland leg (~900–1,000 km class). Cape Town is a longer and more indirect inland leg for a Gauteng/Gaborone-bound box; Walvis Bay is a genuine 1,482 km road haul to Gaborone and no India–Walvis Bay container service could be verified, so for newsprint into Gaborone it is most likely a distraction rather than an option. **Critically, no live India→Southern Africa ocean freight rate per 20ft/40ft could be verified from a carrier or index for September 2026** — the only hard September 2026 carrier-published number found on this lane is a Durban congestion surcharge.

### Cited Findings

**Route 1 — India → Durban (the mainstream option)**

- Maersk has explicitly extended its **Congestion Fee Destination (CFD) for Durban to Indian Sub-Continent origins**: the scope is *"Far East Asia, Indian Sub-Continent, and Middle East"* destined for Durban, applied **regardless of which terminal the container is discharged at**. Amounts: **USD 250 per 20ft, USD 500 per 40ft**. Effective **15 September 2026** for non-regulated countries (Vietnam 18 September 2026, Korea 3 October 2026) — [Maersk, Congestion Fee Destination Durban, 4 Sep 2026](https://www.maersk.com/news/articles/2026/09/04/congestion-fee-destination-durban); companion notice [Maersk, 31 Aug 2026](https://www.maersk.com/news/articles/2026/08/31/congestion-fee-destination-durban-south-africa).
  - **This is a hard, dated, carrier-published number and must go straight into the landed-cost model as a line item on every India-origin box to Durban.**
- Transit time Nhava Sheva (JNPT) → Durban: **~19 days FCL, ~21 days LCL** — [SeaRates, Nhava Sheva to Durban](https://www.searates.com/freight-routes/nhava-sheva-in-to-durban-za) (via search result summary; the page itself returned 403 on direct fetch).
- **Conflicting transit time:** scheduled container ships depart **2–4 times a week** from Nhava Sheva and arrive Durban *"around 12 days 18 hours later"* — [Fluent Cargo, Mumbai to Durban](https://www.fluentcargo.com/routes/mumbai-in/durban-za). **The conflict (≈13 days vs ≈19 days) is material and is shown rather than resolved.** The ~13-day figure most plausibly reflects a direct port-pair sea leg; the ~19-day figure more plausibly reflects real-world door schedules including transhipment.
- Third conflicting range: **18–28 days** from Indian ports to Cape Town **or** Durban — [JLog, Shipping from India to South Africa 2026](https://jlog.co.za/ship-from-india-to-south-africa/).
- Carriers named as operating Nhava Sheva–Durban: **ONE, Maersk, MSC, Evergreen, COSCO, Hapag-Lloyd**, with **2–4 departures per week** — [SeaRates / Fluent Cargo route pages, via search](https://www.fluentcargo.com/routes/mumbai-in/durban-za). CMA CGM and PIL were **not** confirmed on this lane by any source retrieved.
- ONE publishes a Middle East service schedule that touches the India–Middle East network — [ONE Middle East Service PDF](https://www.one-line.com/sites/oneglobal/files/2025-01/Middle%20East%20Service.pdf) — but no India–South Africa string was extracted from it.

**Route 2 — India → Cape Town**

- **ESTIMATE, NOT A LIVE QUOTATION:** a 20ft container from **Mumbai or Chennai to Cape Town** is quoted in a South African forwarder's 2026 guide at **ZAR 14,000 – ZAR 28,000** (roughly ZAR 950–1,850 per CBM), described as typically cheaper than the China–South Africa equivalent — [JLog, 2026](https://jlog.co.za/ship-from-india-to-south-africa/). The same page states rates are otherwise *"on request"* and gives **no 40ft figure, no THC, no documentation fee, no cargo dues**.
- Same source: freight to **Cape Town or Durban takes 18–28 days** depending on origin and routing — [JLog](https://jlog.co.za/ship-from-india-to-south-africa/).
- **Route 2's inland leg is the problem, not the sea leg.** GABCON (the Gaborone rail dry port) receives **over 98% of its freight from Durban**, with only *"smaller volumes from Port Elizabeth and Cape Town"* — [Freight News, Gabcon developing regional container hub](https://www.freightnews.co.za/article/gabcon-developing-regional-container-hub). Cape Town is a served-but-thin option into Gaborone by rail.

**Route 3 — India → Walvis Bay → Gaborone (assessment: probably a distraction)**

- Port of Walvis Bay's new container terminal was built on reclaimed land (construction from mid-2014), **commissioned 24 August 2019**, with capacity of **at least 750,000 TEU per annum** — [Namport, Port of Walvis Bay's New Container Terminal](https://www.namport.com.na/news/267/Port-of-Walvis-Bay-s-New-Container-Terminal/); [Namport Container Terminal](https://www.namport.com.na/port-engineering/container-terminal/592/).
- Walvis Bay overall handles *"899 vessels and about 8 million tonnes of cargo per annum"* and is positioned as a fast route between Southern Africa and **Europe, the Far East and the Americas** — [Namport, Welcome to the Port of Walvis Bay](https://www.namport.com.na/ports/welcome-to-the-port-of-walvis-bay/522/).
- **Road distance Walvis Bay → Gaborone: 1,482 km** — [Trippy, Walvis Bay to Gaborone driving distance](https://www.trippy.com/distance/Walvis-Bay-to-Gaborone). The planned Trans-Kalahari **railway** connecting Walvis Bay to Gaborone is quoted at **1,500 km** — [Ecofin Agency, Trans-Kalahari Railway Corridor Project](https://www.ecofinagency.com/news-infrastructures/0306-47112-trans-kalahari-railway-corridor-project-moves-to-next-phase).
- The Trans-Kalahari Corridor (TKC) runs **Walvis Bay–Windhoek–Gaborone–Johannesburg/Pretoria, over 1,900 km**, was jointly established by Namibia, South Africa and Botswana and **officially opened in 1998**; it *"cuts the distance by 400 kilometres"* versus traditional routes via southern Namibia to Gauteng — [Walvis Bay Corridor Group, Trans-Kalahari Corridor](https://www.wbcg.com.na/trans-kalahari-corridor/).
- A second corridor description confirms the route passes **Lobatse, Gaborone and Francistown** and that using it *"requires a Botswana transit permit and a Namibia cross-border permit"*; Walvis Bay's actual container traffic to Botswana/Zambia/Zimbabwe is dominated by **copper and zinc exports from the Copperbelt**, with imports as backhaul — [Kweli, Major Trucking Corridors of Southern Africa (2026)](https://www.kweli.co.za/blog/trucking-corridors-southern-africa/).
- **No India → Walvis Bay container service was found in any source retrieved.** Namport's own news pages surfaced no India container service announcement.

**Indian load ports — comparison**

- Only **Nhava Sheva / JNPT** could be confirmed as having a dense, multi-carrier, 2–4 sailings/week service to Durban ([Fluent Cargo](https://www.fluentcargo.com/routes/mumbai-in/durban-za)). **Mundra, Chennai, Kolkata, Visakhapatnam, Tuticorin and Kandla were not verifiable** against Southern Africa sailings or rates from any source retrieved — see Gaps.
- Chennai appears alongside Mumbai as an origin in the one indicative rate band found ([JLog](https://jlog.co.za/ship-from-india-to-south-africa/)), which implies commercial service exists from Chennai, but no frequency, carrier list or transit time was published.
- An Indian-side terminal tariff exists in published form for reference — [Transworld Terminals, Nhava Sheva tariff Rev 2 Yr 2026/27, rates in INR per 20' (TEU) and 40' (FEU)](https://transworld-terminals.com/images/nhavasheva_tariff.pdf) — but the content could not be extracted and the specific INR amounts are **UNKNOWN** from this research.

**Market context (global, not lane-specific)**

- Drewry World Container Index (composite, global 40ft): **USD 4,465 on 3 September 2026**; **USD 4,476 on 10 September 2026** (second consecutive stable week); **USD 4,500 on 17 September 2026 (+1%)** — [Drewry WCI](https://www.drewry.co.uk/trackers-and-indices/latest-trackers-and-indices/world-container-index-assessed-by-drewry); [DCN, WCI 10 September 2026](https://www.thedcn.com.au/news/world-container-index-10-september-2026). **Drewry's WCI does not include an India–South Africa leg**, so this is background only.
- For a sanity anchor on the *destination* side of the same ocean: **China → South Africa, July 2026: USD 2,403–2,937 per 20GP and USD 3,096–3,784 per 40GP into Durban** — [Sino Shipping, Freight China–South Africa, Sep 2026 update](https://www.sino-shipping.com/freight-china-south-africa/). India is generally cheaper than China on this lane because *"India is geographically closer"* and *"competition on India–SA routes is higher"* — [JLog](https://jlog.co.za/ship-from-india-to-south-africa/).
- Seasonality warning: peak season for ocean rates runs **mid-August to mid-October** — [JLog](https://jlog.co.za/ship-from-india-to-south-africa/). **September 2026 quotations therefore sit inside the peak window** and should not be annualised.
- 2026 outlook: rates are expected to remain **range-bound with periodic spikes** driven by capacity withdrawals, port congestion or geopolitical events — [iContainers, Freight Rate Forecast 2026](https://www.icontainers.com/freight-rate-forecast-2026/).

### Inferences

- The existence of a Maersk Durban congestion surcharge **newly extended to Indian Sub-Continent origins with effect from 15 September 2026** is a direct signal that Durban was congested in Q3 2026 and that carriers were repricing the destination. A buyer building a landed-cost model for a September 2026 start should assume **USD 250/20ft or USD 500/40ft of congestion surcharge on top of base ocean freight** for Maersk, and should assume competitors will follow with similar or identical fees (they historically do), while confirming each carrier individually.
- Because the surcharge is **per container and flat**, it is roughly **twice as expensive per box on a 40ft as on a 20ft, but not twice as expensive per tonne** — and since paper reels are weight-limited rather than volume-limited (section C), the surcharge lands more heavily per tonne on a 20ft than on a 40ft. The two effects partly cancel; this must be modelled per tonne, not per box.
- Route 3 (Walvis Bay) fails on two counts simultaneously: no verified India service, and a **1,482 km road leg to Gaborone** versus the Durban routing which feeds a **dedicated rail dry port inside Gaborone** (GABCON, section B). For a 2–3 box/month newsprint programme with no Namibian backhaul, Walvis Bay is very likely a distraction. It would only become interesting if (a) a direct India–Walvis Bay call existed, and (b) Durban congestion persisted at the level implied by the September 2026 surcharge.
- Route 2 (Cape Town) is structurally disadvantaged for Gaborone: the sea leg is not meaningfully cheaper (the one indicative band found covers Cape Town and Durban together), while the inland leg is longer and the rail service to GABCON thinner (<2% of GABCON volume). Cape Town is a contingency routing, not a base case.
- Nhava Sheva/JNPT should be the **default load port assumption** in the model until a specific mill's nearest port is known, because it is the only Indian port with verified multi-carrier weekly frequency to Durban. If the mill is in Tamil Nadu, Chennai or Tuticorin may be shorter door-to-port, but the buyer must obtain sailing schedules rather than assume parity.

## B. SOUTH AFRICA → BOTSWANA (inland leg, rail vs road, borders, customs)

### Takeaway
There is a **purpose-built rail solution into Gaborone** — GABCON, a Botswana Railways / Transnet Freight Rail joint-venture dry port — which is fed overwhelmingly from Durban, and Transnet's own Durban terminal tariff grants **10 free storage days to rail containers destined "over border"** versus 3 days back-of-port. That is a real, quantified advantage for Botswana-bound boxes. The countervailing risk is the **border**, where a Botswana customs procedure change produced queues of up to 10 km and waits of up to four days. **No trucking rate for any South African port → Gaborone lane could be verified**, and neither Transnet Freight Rail nor Botswana Railways publishes a container tariff publicly.

### B1. Rail — GABCON (Gaborone Container Terminal)

### Cited Findings

- **GABCON was established in 1999 to operate as an international container terminal business in Gaborone**; it is a **joint venture between Botswana Railways (majority shareholder) and South Africa's Transnet Freight Rail** — [Botswana Railways, GABCON](https://www.botswanarailways.co.bw/gabcon-0).
- GABCON is a **dry port** that *"facilitates all rail transported containerized cargo into and out of Botswana"*, with rail links to **four South African ports: Cape Town, Durban, Port Elizabeth and East London** — [Botswana Railways, GABCON](https://www.botswanarailways.co.bw/gabcon-0).
- Operating detail (from a Freight News feature; **see date caveat below**): **over 98% of freight arrives from Durban**, smaller volumes from Port Elizabeth and Cape Town; **five trains per week** (up from two); **train turnaround within four hours** (MD Modise Koofhethile); **up to 200 TEU per day**; storage for **800 containers**; **~1,500 TEU per month**; able to handle **two 45-wagon trains simultaneously**; warehousing on site for consolidation and container stuffing/destuffing; **36,000 m² of land** available for expansion including a planned car terminal; Botswana customs planned to open offices inside GABCON with scanner facilities in development — [Freight News, Gabcon developing regional container hub](https://www.freightnews.co.za/article/gabcon-developing-regional-container-hub).
  - **Date caveat:** this article references GABCON *"becoming private in April 2009"* and names a managing director; it is therefore probably **several years old** and the throughput/frequency figures may not describe 2026. Treat as directional, not current.
- **Botswana Railways began operating through freight services from South Africa to Gaborone on 19 May** (year not captured in the retrieved summary), explicitly *"to reduce operational inefficiencies and delays caused by transhipment at the border and to mitigate locomotive availability issues"* — [Railway Gazette, Botswana](https://www.railwaygazette.com/category/botswana/); see also [Railway Gazette, World rail freight news round-up, 1 June 2026](https://www.railwaygazette.com/freight/2026/06/01/world-rail-freight-news-round-up-8/).
- *"Rail is more cost-effective than road on the route"* — GABCON managing director Modise Koofhethile, [Freight News](https://www.freightnews.co.za/article/gabcon-developing-regional-container-hub). **No tariff figures accompany this claim.**

### B2. Durban terminal charges actually payable on a Botswana-bound box

These are **hard, dated figures extracted from the official terminal tariff book** (Durban Gateway Terminal (Pty) Ltd, the Transnet Port Terminals entity at Durban; registration 2025/929823/07), **tariffs effective 1 January 2026 until 31 March 2026**, all amounts in **ZAR** — [DGT Tariff Book 1 January 2026](https://cdnweb.ictsi.com/s3fs-public/2026-01/DGT%20Tariff%20Book%201%20January%202026.pdf).

- **Terminal handling (landing/shipping), normal containers: R2,652 / R3,917 / R5,183 per container** across the three size columns.
  - *Extraction caveat:* the column headers were lost in PDF text extraction. The tariff book's own container-size table lists sizes as **13.7 m, 12 m and 6 m** (all rated max mass 30,480 kg). TPT tariff tables conventionally run smallest-to-largest, so the most likely reading is **6 m (20ft) = R2,652; 12 m (40ft/40HC) = R3,917; 13.7 m (45ft) = R5,183**. **This mapping is an inference and must be confirmed against the rendered PDF before use in a model.**
- Empty containers (imports and exports only): **R2,266 / R3,347 / R4,433**.
- Abnormal containers: **R3,709 / R5,500 / R7,286**. IMDG containers (all classes): same, **R3,709 / R5,500 / R7,286**.
- Transhipment, normal containers: **R2,652 inwards + R2,652 outwards** (6 m), **R3,917 + R3,917** (12 m), **R5,183 + R5,183** (13.7 m) — i.e. **charged both ways** at the same terminal.
- **Storage of import containers (per container per day or part thereof), excluding abnormal:** *"First 3 days (72 hours) free, the free period is applied from 00h01 on the day after the container is discharged until the container leaves the gate."* **Day 4 (06h00–23h59): R2,533 / R5,067 / R7,606. From day 5 onwards: R4,123 / R8,237 / R12,355.**
- **Storage of import abnormal containers:** Day 4 flat **R7,606** across all sizes; from day 5 onwards **R12,355**.
- **CRITICAL FOR BOTSWANA — rail free-storage differentiation:** *"The free storage period for rail containers will be as follows; Destined to the Back of Port will be 3 days, Destined to City Deep will be 5 days; Destined for Over boarder will be 10 days."* If the customer then decides to remove a rail container **by road** instead, *"the amending and move tariff will be applicable"* and only **3 free days** are granted from the date the amendment was submitted.
- Liability carve-out: if Transnet Freight Rail or a private train operating company causes high dwell time, *"DGT will hold the respective party accountable and will not invoice the Customer."*
- Container size / mass definitions in the same tariff book: **6 m (6,058 mm) and 12 m (12,192 mm) containers both show max mass 30,480 kg**, *"conditional on the container being plate rated to carry that mass"*; **High Cube = same dimensions but 2,896 mm height**.
- Terminal operating hours at DGT: **06h00–18h00 Monday–Friday; 07h00–15h00 Saturday and Sunday**.
- Separate from the tariff book: **Transnet Port Terminals increased its container handling surcharge to R78 per container from 1 June 2026**, to cope with surging diesel prices — [News24, 18 May 2026](https://www.news24.com/business/economy/transnet-port-terminals-raises-container-surcharge-for-june-20260518-0996).
- Transnet's consolidated 2026 tariff book exists at [transnet.net tariff book 2026](https://www.transnet.net/getFile.ashx?id=8701) (not extracted). Transnet Port Terminals' own 2025 book is at [TPT Tariff Book 2025](https://www.transnetportterminals.net/Customer/Tariffs/TPT%20Tariff%20Book%202025.pdf); a separate confirmation that TPT tariffs are *"effective from 1 April 2026"* with the operator reserving the right to amend at its sole discretion appears in the same family of documents.

### B3. Customs: SACU, bonded transit, and why origin matters enormously

### Cited Findings

- **SACU** comprises South Africa and the BLNS states (Botswana, Lesotho, Namibia, Eswatini) — [Southern African Customs Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_African_Customs_Union).
- *"Once goods have cleared customs in one SACU country — at the port of Durban, for instance — they can generally move freely to another member country like Botswana without incurring additional customs duties"* — [Botswana Import Requirements and Documentation](https://www.privacyshield.gov/ps/article?id=Botswana-import-requirements-and-documentation).
- **The SAD 500 is the single customs declaration form** for external trade and is the official declaration document for imports and exports within SACU — [BURS SAD Guide](https://www.burs.org.bw/phocadownload/customs_and_excise_downloads/SAD_Guide.pdf).
- *"In terms of SADC regulations, cargo moving 'In Bond' from South Africa to BLNS (Botswana, Lesotho, Namibia, Swaziland) countries must be delivered to a Customs approved bonded facility"* — [Clearing and Forwarding, Africa Used Vehicle Adviser](https://africausedvehicleadviser.wordpress.com/transporting-used-vehicles-within-africa/).
- SARS has modernised customs so that *"the bulk of clearances"* are electronic, using a **customs notification (CN1)** rather than paper SAD documents — [Customs Modernisation Release 3 – SACU](https://mpoverello.com/2012/02/11/customs-modernisation-release-3-sacu/). **Note this source is dated 2012**; the direction of travel is confirmed but current procedural detail should be verified with SARS/BURS.
- Botswana's customs authority is the **Botswana Unified Revenue Service (BURS)**; import duty and VAT treatment for machinery/equipment imports is described at [PV Know How, Importing Machinery to Botswana: BURS, VAT & Duties](https://www.pvknowhow.com/countries/botswana/import-solar-equipment-botswana/). **Specific HS-code duty rates for newsprint and kraft paper were not researched here** (out of scope of this logistics brief, but note the SACU point below).

### B4. Border crossings and delays

### Cited Findings

- **Skilpadshek (South Africa) / Pioneer Gate (Botswana):** Skilpadshek is on the SA–Botswana border **52 km northwest of Zeerust**; the corresponding Botswana-side post is **Pioneer Gate**; the **Trans-Kalahari Corridor passes through Skilpadshek**, which is the western end of South Africa's **N4 Platinum Highway** — [Skilpadshek, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skilpadshek).
- **A BURS in-transit cargo clearing procedure change caused severe congestion.** A new **Unique Consignment Reference (UCR)** per-item customs declaration procedure, requiring *"each and every item had its own received-in-transit (RIT) line entry"*, produced *"queues and waiting times of up to four days"*. Reported queue at **Skilpadshek: 10 km westbound, later shrinking to 6 km** — [Freight News, New cargo clearing system causes Botswana border backlog](https://www.freightnews.co.za/article/new-cargo-clearing-system-causes-botswana-border-backlog).
- Follow-up reporting (**dated 29 November 2024**): a queue of *"mostly general cargo trucks stretched for more than four kilometres"* at Skilpadshek; *"permits issued for time-sensitive cargo often expired by the time loads finally reached border gates"*; after stakeholder meetings BURS *"acknowledged the impracticality of individual RIT entries and agreed to revert to consolidated general cargo entries per load"*; authorities indicated *"all cargo entering Botswana will henceforth have to be precleared"*; and a persistent structural issue is the **lack of systems integration between Botswana's customs platform and those of neighbouring countries** — [Freight News, Cargo clearing rigidity continues to cause Botswana border congestion](https://www.freightnews.co.za/article/cargo-clearing-rigidity-continues-to-cause-botswana-border-congestion).
- **Kopfontein (SA) / Tlokweng (Botswana)** is separately reported as a persistent problem post for transporters — [Freight News, Kopfontein border remains a headache for transporters](https://www.freightnews.co.za/article/kopfontein-border-remains-a-headache-for-transporters) (article not fetched; headline-level evidence only).
- Trans-Kalahari transit through Botswana *"requires a Botswana transit permit and a Namibia cross-border permit"* — [Kweli, Major Trucking Corridors of Southern Africa (2026)](https://www.kweli.co.za/blog/trucking-corridors-southern-africa/).
- A general guide to the SA–Botswana posts exists at [Drive South Africa, Botswana Border Posts Crossing Guide](https://www.drivesouthafrica.com/blog/botswana-border-posts-crossing-guide/) (not fetched).
- A quantified economic study of Trans-Kalahari border flows exists — [CBRTA, Cross-Border Flow Calculator Economic Impact Assessment Report for the Trans-Kalahari Corridor](https://www.cbrta.co.za/uploads/files/Cross-Border-Flow-Calculator-Economic-Impact-Assessment-Report-for-Trans-Kalahari-Corridor.pdf) — **not fetched; this is the single most promising unexploited source for hard border-delay and cost-of-delay numbers.**

### B5. Road haulage rates

### Cited Findings

- **South Africa's maximum loaded truck weight is 56,000 kg** *"unless a lesser load is specified by the vehicle manufacturer"* — [SANRAL, official account](https://x.com/SANRAL_za/status/1782413515237548085). Weight limits are governed by the **National Road Traffic Act**; enforcement is via **weighbridges, roadside inspections and mobile enforcement units** — [Sumeil, Understanding Legal Weight Limits in South African Transport](https://sumeil.co.za/understanding-legal-weight-limits/).
- Rate-building methodology (no benchmark numbers published): *"A freight rate is built, not looked up"* — **Cost per km × measured road distance + return leg recovery = rate**; the page uses **R20/km purely as an illustration** and explicitly says *"use your own"* — [Kweli, Freight Rates in South Africa (2026)](https://www.kweli.co.za/rates/).
- Same source warns that **straight-line distance estimates are badly wrong**: Durban → Gqeberha is **941 km measured vs 570 km estimated** (off by 371 km); Johannesburg → Gqeberha **1,060 km measured vs 770 km estimated** — [Kweli](https://www.kweli.co.za/rates/). **Implication: do not model Durban→Gaborone from a map straight line.**
- Cross-border rate construction must include *"transit country costs, border contingency days, COMESA Yellow Card coverage, and USD invoicing... as line items"* — [Kweli](https://www.kweli.co.za/rates/). (COMESA Yellow Card is relevant to Zambia/DRC lanes rather than SACU-internal Botswana, but the "border contingency days" principle applies directly.)
- A South African forwarder rate card exists covering **Durban → Johannesburg import rates valid from 1 April** with figures reported in search as **R9,800 – R18,950 depending on vehicle size and zone (2025–2026)** — [Covenant Logistics, Rates and Pricing](https://covenantlogistics.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/CL-Rates-and-Pricing.pdf). **The PDF is image-based and could not be text-extracted, so this band is search-snippet-level only and is for Durban→Johannesburg, NOT Durban→Gaborone. Do not use it as a Gaborone rate.**
- Trans-Kalahari corridor distance reference: **Johannesburg → Walvis Bay approximately 1,950 km**, routing via **Lobatse, Gaborone and Francistown** — [Kweli, Major Trucking Corridors of Southern Africa (2026)](https://www.kweli.co.za/blog/trucking-corridors-southern-africa/).

### Inferences

- **The "over boarder" 10-day free-storage rule in the Durban tariff book is a real and quantifiable reason to move Botswana-bound boxes by rail to GABCON rather than by road out of Durban.** At the published day-5-onward storage rate of **R4,123/day for a 6 m box and R8,237/day for a 12 m box**, seven extra free days is worth roughly **R29k (20ft) to R58k (40ft) of avoided storage exposure per container** in a congested-port scenario. This is not a saving that materialises in a smooth month — it is **insurance against exactly the congestion that Maersk's September 2026 Durban surcharge is pricing**. (Arithmetic is mine; the daily rates are cited.)
- The same rule contains a trap: switching a rail-nominated box to road collapses the free period back to **3 days** and adds an amendment-and-move charge. A buyer must **decide rail-vs-road before nomination**, not after the vessel discharges.
- **Richards Bay → Gaborone should be treated as not applicable for containers until proven otherwise.** Richards Bay appears nowhere in GABCON's rail-linked port list (which names Durban, Cape Town, Port Elizabeth, East London) and nowhere in the container sources retrieved. Richards Bay is primarily a bulk port; asserting a container service there would be speculation. **Flagged as a gap rather than an option.**
- **The SACU point is the decisive commercial asymmetry.** Paper sourced from a South African mill is SACU-origin: it moves to Gaborone on a domestic-style SACU movement with no customs duty and no bonded-transit obligation. Paper sourced from India is third-country origin: it must either be cleared into SACU at Durban and then move duty-paid, or move **in bond** from Durban to a customs-approved bonded facility in Botswana. The in-bond route is what exposes the cargo to the BURS RIT/UCR transit procedure that generated the four-day border waits. **South African sourcing therefore avoids both an import-duty line and the highest-variance delay risk in the whole chain — before any freight arithmetic is done.**
- Conversely, the in-bond route's compliance burden argues strongly for using **GABCON as the bonded destination** (Botswana customs offices and scanner were planned on site) rather than clearing at a road border gate, since the dry port converts a queue-at-the-gate problem into a terminal-processing problem.
- The border-delay evidence is **two years old (late 2024)** and BURS agreed to revert to consolidated per-load entries. It would be unsound to model four-day waits as the September 2026 norm — but equally unsound to model zero contingency. A **border contingency allowance** is the right treatment, consistent with the corridor-rate methodology cited.

## C. CONTAINER LOADING FOR PAPER REELS — payload, not cube, is the constraint

### Takeaway
For newsprint and kraft reels the binding constraint is **mass**, and it binds in a cascade: container plate rating → then the **South African 56,000 kg gross combination limit** on the road leg → and possibly a Botswana limit that could not be sourced. A 20ft box carries **more** payload than a 40ft because both share the same ~30,480 kg maximum gross mass while the 40ft's tare is ~1.5 t heavier. **However, the single most important number for this brief — tonnes of newsprint reels realistically loaded per container — could not be sourced from any paper-industry, TAPPI or carrier document, and is reported as a gap rather than estimated.**

### C1. Container mass figures — and a genuine source conflict

### Cited Findings

- **Official terminal tariff book (highest-authority source found):** container sizes **6,058 mm (6 m / 20ft)** and **12,192 mm (12 m / 40ft)** both show **max mass 30,480 kg**, with the explicit qualifier *"The maximum mass reflected is conditional on the container being plate rated to carry that mass."* High Cube = same dimensions at **2,896 mm height** — [DGT Tariff Book 1 January 2026](https://cdnweb.ictsi.com/s3fs-public/2026-01/DGT%20Tariff%20Book%201%20January%202026.pdf).
- **20ft:** tare ≈ **2,280 kg**, max gross ≈ **30,480 kg**, **payload ≈ 26,730 kg** — [FreightAmigo, 20ft & 40ft Container Weights](https://www.freightamigo.com/en/blog/logistics/essential-guide-to-20-foot-container-specifications-and-loading/); [ContainerMath, Container Weight Limits & Max Payload](https://containermath.com/blog/container-weight-limits-max-payload).
- **40ft:** tare ≈ **3,800 kg**, **payload ≈ 26,680 kg**, *"because both share the ~30,480 kg max gross weight set by the ISO standard, but the 40ft's heavier tare leaves less room for cargo"* — [ContainerMath](https://containermath.com/blog/container-weight-limits-max-payload).
- **CONFLICTING South African source:** a South African container dealer states **20ft max gross weight 24,000 kg**, tare 2,200–2,400 kg, **maximum payload 21,600–21,800 kg**; and **40ft max gross 30,480 kg**, tare 3,700–4,200 kg, **payload 26,280–27,780 kg**; 40HC *"similar gross weight limits but may have slightly higher tare weights"* — [Aquacat Container Traders, Maximum Weight Limits for 20ft and 40ft Containers in South Africa](https://aquacatcontainertraders.co.za/maximum-weight-limits-for-20ft-and-40ft-containers-in-south-africa/).
  - **This conflict is real and must be shown, not resolved by preference.** The 24,000 kg figure for a 20ft is the **older ISO 1496 rating still carried on many in-service 20ft boxes' CSC plates**; 30,480 kg is the **current standard rating**. The tariff book's phrase *"conditional on the container being plate rated to carry that mass"* is precisely the reconciliation: **the box in front of you may be a 24,000 kg unit or a 30,480 kg unit, and only its CSC plate says which.** A landed-cost model for heavy paper **must not assume 30,480 kg without carrier confirmation of plate rating.**
- A further variant figure appears in search results — *"a 20ft container is rated at about 33 cubic metres and a maximum payload around 28 tonnes"* — [Quickfit, Weight of Container 20ft](https://www.quickfitcontaineraccessories.co.uk/blogs/news/weight-of-container-20-ft). **A third distinct payload number for the same box type; shown for completeness as evidence of how unreliable generic container-spec pages are.**
- General principle, same South African source: *"the maximum payload and road weight limits often mean you cannot use the container's full cubic capacity when shipping heavy goods"*, and *"For a standard truck and trailer combination, the legal gross vehicle mass (GVM) is often less than the container's maximum gross weight, especially for 40ft containers"* — [Aquacat](https://aquacatcontainertraders.co.za/maximum-weight-limits-for-20ft-and-40ft-containers-in-south-africa/).

### C2. The road-weight cascade

### Cited Findings

- **South Africa: maximum loaded weight of a truck is 56,000 kg** unless the manufacturer specifies less — [SANRAL](https://x.com/SANRAL_za/status/1782413515237548085).
- Enforcement is real: **weighbridges, roadside inspections, mobile enforcement units** — [Sumeil](https://sumeil.co.za/understanding-legal-weight-limits/).
- GVM is defined as *"the maximum legal mass of a single vehicle, including the vehicle itself, fuel, driver, and payload"*; limits sit under the National Road Traffic Act — [Sumeil](https://sumeil.co.za/understanding-legal-weight-limits/). Permissible maximum combination mass is discussed at [Focus on Transport, Understanding permissible maximum combination mass](https://focusontransport.co.za/understanding-permissible-maximum-combination-mass-2/) (not fetched).
- Country-by-country container and truck weight limits are compiled at [Truck Packer, Global Weight Restrictions guide](https://www.truckpacker.com/newsletter/navigating-global-weight-restrictions-a-complete-guide-to-truck-and-container-weight-limits-by) (not fetched — **a promising source for the missing Botswana limit**).

### C3. Reel orientation, stowage and dunnage practice

### Cited Findings

- **Vertical (eye-to-sky) loading of paper reels:** *"always distribute the load evenly, stow the rolls tightly and fill any spaces between the rolls with airbags or empty pallets"*; and *"when loading rolls of different diameters, load them alternately"* — [Joloda, A Guide to Safe Paper Reel Handling Equipment](https://www.joloda.com/en/news/joloda-transport-a-guide-to-safe-paper-reel-handling-equipment/).
- **Horizontal (lying) reels:** *"When loading or unloading large lying rolls, always use wedges to stop them rolling around."* — [Joloda](https://www.joloda.com/en/news/joloda-transport-a-guide-to-safe-paper-reel-handling-equipment/).
- **Handling discipline:** *"Always use appropriate roll handling equipment and never roll the reels around or push them on the floor of the vehicle."* Post-loading: *"always check you've secured the loads and ensure the cover is watertight"*, with tailboards/sideboards closed and locked and clasps in good condition — [Joloda](https://www.joloda.com/en/news/joloda-transport-a-guide-to-safe-paper-reel-handling-equipment/).
- **Dunnage bag practice:** *"inflate the bags to fill any void between the products and the container walls"* — [Breckpack, Dunnage Air Bags Guide](https://breckpack.com/dunnage-air-bags-explained-safer-trailer-loads-with-the-right-inflation-and-placement/); dunnage bags/airbags are used *"to secure and stabilize cargo"* — [Dunnage bag, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunnage_bag).
- **General container-stowage rule:** *"Cargo stowed in containers must be secured on all sides to prevent shifting, in particular towards the door."* — [Tata Steel, Stowage and securing on seagoing vessels](https://products.tatasteelnederland.com/sites/producttsn/files/Stowing%20and%20securing%20on%20seagoing%20vessels.pdf).
- **Terminology confirmation:** *"ETTS stands for 'Eye To The Sky'"* and refers to coils positioned so that **the bore is vertical**, placed on skids — [Tata Steel](https://products.tatasteelnederland.com/sites/producttsn/files/Stowing%20and%20securing%20on%20seagoing%20vessels.pdf). Analogous eye-to-sky vs eye-to-side securement trade-offs for cylindrical rolled cargo are set out at [ATS, Safely Shipping Metal Coils](https://www.atsinc.com/blog/shipping-metal-coils).
- **Dense-cylindrical-cargo dunnage principle:** *"dunnage should be placed under each top layer of coils to achieve stability, with dunnage placed between each of the coils"* — [Tata Steel](https://products.tatasteelnederland.com/sites/producttsn/files/Stowing%20and%20securing%20on%20seagoing%20vessels.pdf). **This is metal-coil guidance, cited by analogy because no equivalent paper-reel marine stowage document could be sourced. Flagged as analogy, not as paper-specific authority.**
- Definition: dunnage is *"inexpensive or waste material used to load and secure cargo during transportation"* — [Dunnage, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunnage); see also [Cargo Handbook, Dunnage](https://www.cargohandbook.com/Dunnage).
- Automated reel-handling systems for newspaper print rooms are specified around **30-tonne batches of print rolls** — [Bardawil / Joloda newsprint handling](http://www.bardawil.ae/joloda.php). **This is a print-room conveyor capacity figure, NOT a container capacity figure, and must not be read as tonnes per container.**

### Inferences

- **The 20ft is the correct default box for newsprint and kraft reels, and the reason is arithmetic, not convention.** Both 20ft and 40ft share the same ~30,480 kg maximum gross mass, while the 40ft's tare is roughly 1,500 kg heavier. The 20ft therefore offers **more payload than the 40ft** (≈26,730 kg vs ≈26,680 kg on the cited generic figures, and the gap widens on real tares). For a cargo that hits the weight limit long before it fills the cube, **paying for a 40ft buys air.** (Derived from the cited tare/payload figures; the 40HC adds cube and a little more tare, so it is the worst of the three for weight-limited paper.)
- **The 40HC is actively counterproductive for reels.** Its only advantage is the extra 305 mm of interior height (2,896 mm external height per the tariff book). Newsprint reels loaded eye-to-sky are typically well under that height in a single tier, and a second tier is a mass problem, not a height problem. **The 40HC's higher tare reduces payload for no usable benefit.**
- **The binding constraint is a cascade, and the model must apply the *lowest* number in it:**
  1. **CSC plate rating of the actual container** — 30,480 kg on a modern box, but possibly **24,000 kg** on an older 20ft ([Aquacat](https://aquacatcontainertraders.co.za/maximum-weight-limits-for-20ft-and-40ft-containers-in-south-africa/) vs [ContainerMath](https://containermath.com/blog/container-weight-limits-max-payload) — the conflict is not academic, it is a ~5 t swing in loadable tonnage).
  2. **Minus tare** (2,280–2,400 kg for 20ft; 3,700–4,200 kg for 40ft) to get payload.
  3. **Minus the South African road limit**: total combination gross mass ≤ **56,000 kg** ([SANRAL](https://x.com/SANRAL_za/status/1782413515237548085)). A tractor-and-skeletal combination's own mass must be deducted before the box's payload; this is what the Aquacat source means by *"the legal GVM is often less than the container's maximum gross weight, especially for 40ft containers."*
  4. **Minus any Botswana limit** — **which could not be sourced and may be lower.**
  5. **Minus floor-loading / point-loading limits** — see gap below.
- **Because step 1 is uncertain and step 4 is unknown, any single "tonnes per container" figure produced today would be fabricated.** The correct action for the buyer is to **obtain the CSC plate rating and the carrier's stated maximum payload for the specific equipment nominated, plus a Botswana axle-load confirmation, before fixing a tonnage per box.** That is a two-email exercise and it changes the freight-cost-per-tonne materially.
- **Reel geometry matters through floor footprint, not cube.** Eye-to-sky loading puts the full reel mass onto a circular footprint, concentrating load; reel **diameter** sets how many footprints fit across the 2,438 mm internal width and along the length, and reel **width** (i.e. reel height when eye-to-sky) sets whether tiering is even geometrically possible. Loading on the side spreads mass along a line contact but requires wedging against rolling ([Joloda](https://www.joloda.com/en/news/joloda-transport-a-guide-to-safe-paper-reel-handling-equipment/)) and wastes the interstitial volume between adjacent cylinders. **Eye-to-sky is the standard for containerised reels and is what the cited guidance addresses first.**
- **Practical stowage recipe supportable from the cited sources:** load eye-to-sky; distribute mass evenly fore-and-aft to protect axle loads on the road leg; stow reels tight; alternate diameters when mixed; fill all voids — especially the void toward the doors — with **inflated dunnage bags or empty pallets**; brace against the door end specifically ([Tata Steel](https://products.tatasteelnederland.com/sites/producttsn/files/Stowing%20and%20securing%20on%20seagoing%20vessels.pdf)); never roll or push reels on the floor; verify the container is watertight, since newsprint and kraft are both moisture-sensitive and a wet reel is a total loss of that reel.
- **Kraft paper reels:** no kraft-specific loading data was found. Physically kraft reels are the same problem class as newsprint — dense cylindrical rolls, weight-limited — so the same cascade and the same 20ft preference apply. **Kraft grammages are typically higher than newsprint's, which if anything makes the weight constraint bind sooner, not later.** For **sheets on pallets**, the constraint shifts: palletised sheets stack squarely, use the cube far more efficiently than cylinders, and are also dense — so palletised sheets will also be weight-limited, but with far less wasted interstitial volume. **All of this is reasoning from the cited weight mechanics, not from a sourced kraft loading document.**
- **One box per month of kraft plus 2–3 of newsprint is a small programme.** At that volume the buyer will not get carrier-specific equipment guarantees and should expect to accept whatever 20ft boxes the carrier allocates — which reinforces the need to know the **plate-rating spread** rather than a single assumed payload.

### Gaps

- **THE CENTRAL GAP: tonnes of newsprint reels realistically loaded per 20ft / 40ft / 40HC container — UNKNOWN / NOT SOURCED.** No paper-industry, TAPPI, mill or carrier document giving a tonnes-per-container figure for newsprint or kraft reels was found. A search-result snippet asserted that paper reels are *"heavy but small-volume"* and *"almost always shipped in a 20ft container"*, but **it could not be attributed to a specific verified page and is therefore not cited as a finding.** The one "30 tons of print rolls" figure found refers to a print-room unloading conveyor, not a container.

### Inferences

- **The ocean leg is the only stage with usable numbers, and even it spans ~13 to 28 days.** The spread is not noise: the low end is a port-pair sea passage, the high end is a door schedule with transhipment. A model should carry the ocean leg as a **range**, not a point, and should separately note that **sailing frequency of 2–4 per week adds up to ~3–4 days of booking latency** before the vessel even leaves.
- **The ocean leg alone therefore plausibly spans roughly 2 to 4.5 weeks port-to-port**, before any origin production time, any port-side dwell, any inland leg and any border time. The remaining stages are unsourced, so the honest statement is: **India→Gaborone is a multi-week to two-month-class pipeline whose total cannot be pinned down from public data, and the buyer must obtain (a) mill production lead time, (b) a forwarder's door-to-door schedule, and (c) a current border-dwell read before committing to a replenishment cycle.**
- **The single largest variance in the chain is the border, not the ocean.** A four-day border event is small against a 19-day sea leg in absolute terms, but it is the stage with (i) no published normal duration, (ii) a documented history of sudden procedural shocks, and (iii) a dependency on a customs IT integration that is explicitly described as lacking. **This is the stage to buy contingency against.**
- **Rail via GABCON materially de-risks the timeline, not just the cost.** Ten free storage days at Durban for over-border rail cargo ([DGT Tariff Book](https://cdnweb.ictsi.com/s3fs-public/2026-01/DGT%20Tariff%20Book%201%20January%202026.pdf)) means a rail-nominated box can absorb a week of downstream disruption at zero storage cost, whereas a road-nominated box starts burning **R4,123/day (20ft)** from day 5. For a buyer running only 3–4 boxes a month with no buffer stock, that free time is the cheapest inventory insurance available on this lane.
- **For a South African-origin alternative, stages 1–6 of the table collapse entirely** — no ocean leg, no port dwell, no in-bond transit, no third-country clearance. Combined with the SACU duty position (section B3), **South African sourcing is a fundamentally shorter and lower-variance pipeline, and the India comparison must be made on landed cost *and* on working capital tied up in a much longer, more variable pipeline** — not on ocean freight per tonne alone.
