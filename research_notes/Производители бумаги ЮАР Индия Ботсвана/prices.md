# Current Market Prices — Newsprint & Kraft Paper, FOB India / FOB South Africa (as of September 2026)

> **READ THIS BEFORE USING ANY NUMBER BELOW.**
>
> 1. **No public price reporting agency publishes an "FOB India" or "FOB South Africa" export price for newsprint or kraft paper.** Fastmarkets RISI/FOEX, EUWID and PIX cover Europe, North America, China/Asia and (newly) *domestic ex-works India*. They do **not** publish FOB Indian or FOB South African export assessments for these grades. Everything below labelled FOB is therefore **DERIVED or ESTIMATED**, never a published index.
> 2. Every figure is tagged: `[INDEX]` = published index/assessment, `[REPORTED]` = reported transaction/customs/company figure, `[LIST]` = published list or offer price, `[ESTIMATE]` = my derivation, **NOT A LIVE QUOTATION**, `[OUTDATED]` = freshest available but older than Sept 2026.
> 3. **Currency conversions.** Indian source data is in ₹/kg or ₹/MT. I could not verify a September 2026 USD/INR spot rate from a source in this research. All INR→USD conversions below use **₹88.00 = US$1 as an explicit working assumption — UNVERIFIED FX**. The buyer must re-run every conversion at the spot rate on quotation day. The INR figures are the real data; the USD figures are arithmetic on an assumed rate.
> 4. Several Lesprom customs-statistics figures below were visible only in search-engine snippets of Lesprom pages; when I fetched the pages directly they returned "we don't have data for this query yet." I have flagged these as `[REPORTED — snippet only, page not verifiable]`. They are directionally consistent with each other and with the Indian landed-price list, but the buyer should verify against ITC TradeMap or DGCIS before relying on them.

## 1. NEWSPRINT — export price levels USD/MT: FOB India, FOB South Africa, plus regional cross-checks

### Takeaway
There is **no FOB India newsprint export price**, because India is structurally a large net *importer* of newsprint (world's second-largest consumer, ~1.3 mt/yr, domestic capacity insufficient) — Indian mills sell domestically, not into export. The only hard, current, India-linked newsprint number is the **CIF India import unit value, which rose sharply through 2026 to US$668/MT in June 2026 and US$694.5/MT in July 2026**. FOB South Africa newsprint is **UNKNOWN** — no published assessment and no usable customs unit value was obtainable.

- **Derived FOB-origin newsprint level for September 2026 `[ESTIMATE — NOT A LIVE QUOTATION]`:** working back from the July 2026 CIF India unit value of US$694.5/MT and deducting ocean freight + insurance for typical Asia/Europe→India newsprint routes, an FOB-origin level in the **US$600–660/MT** region is implied. This is an inference from one customs average across all origins and grades; the freight deduction itself is unsourced here. Do not quote it to a mill.

- **`[ESTIMATE — NOT A LIVE QUOTATION]` Indicative FOB India, recycled testliner/kraft liner, 120–150 gsm, 18–22 BF, container quantities, September 2026: US$440–520/MT FOB west-coast India.** Built from: domestic 12 BF ex-mill ₹36–38/kg (≈US$409–432/MT), plus the observed ₹7–9/kg premium that one mill charges for "export grade" (₹43–47/kg ≈ US$489–534/MT), cross-checked against ChemAnalyst's ≈US$440/MT India level. Treat as a sanity band only; the export-grade premium is from a single mill line item.
- **`[ESTIMATE]` A 2–3 BF step up (18→22 BF) should add only ~US$6–25/MT.** If a supplier asks a large premium for 22 BF over 18 BF, that is a negotiating position, not a cost reality.
- **Virgin kraft / sack kraft commands a large premium over recycled testliner.** No Indian or South African sack-kraft price was obtainable, but the direction is unambiguous: virgin kraft is a pulp-cost product (NBSK list US$1,570/MT — §4) whereas recycled testliner is an OCC-cost product (OCC ≈US$100/short ton — §4). **Expect sack kraft to price at a multiple of recycled testliner, not a small premium.** Any offer pricing "virgin sack kraft" close to testliner levels is almost certainly recycled or blended — demand a fibre-furnish declaration and a Cobb/tensile test certificate.
- **White-top carries a very large premium over brown recycled:** €660–680 vs €450–480 delivered Poland at the same 140 g, i.e. **roughly +45% / +€190–210/MT.** That ratio is a reasonable cross-market guide for quoting white-top against brown from any origin.
- **Brown kraftliner and recycled testliner are diverging as of September 2026:** virgin kraftliner is being pushed up (SCA +€100/t effective 1 Sept 2026; white-top stable), while recycled fluting is slipping (semi-chem fluting −€20/t in August; PIX GCC testliner and fluting down). A buyer should therefore expect **firmer virgin quotes and softer recycled quotes** and should not accept a "the whole market is rising" framing across both.

### Inferences

The practical route is: take the Indian **ex-works** benchmark (Fastmarkets India recycled containerboard, or the Indian Printer & Publisher list), then add inland haulage to Nhava Sheva/Mundra, port/THC, documentation and export margin to reach FOB. Those adders must come from a freight forwarder, not from a price index.
- For South Africa the comparison is different in kind: Gauteng/KZN → Gaborone is **road/rail, not sea**, so the relevant South African basis is **ex-mill or delivered Gaborone**, and asking a South African mill for "FOB" may produce a confused quote. Ask South African suppliers for **ex-works** and **DAP Gaborone** in ZAR, and Indian suppliers for **FOB Indian port** and **CFR Durban/Walvis Bay** in USD.
- **Trap to avoid:** the IndexBox "South Africa paper exports US$3,324/ton" figure is the kind of number that looks authoritative and would overstate a kraft benchmark by ~5×. It is an all-HS-48 aggregate.

### Gaps
- Sea freight and inland-transit cost components for India→Durban/Walvis Bay→Gaborone: not researched here (outside this assignment's price scope).

## 7. Payment terms in the paper export trade — and whether Indian and South African mills differ

### Takeaway
The Indian export norm for paper is an **irrevocable LC at sight from a top-100 prime bank**, and Indian domestic kraft sales can be **strictly cash / no credit**. ### Inferences
- **`[ESTIMATE — NOT SOURCED, a working planning assumption only]` A 2–3 container/month buyer should plan for a premium in the region of 5–15% over the index/large-contract level**, on the reasoning that: (a) the observed intra-grade spread in a single Indian mill's own list is ~19% for the same BF, and a small buyer lands in the upper part of any such range rather than the lower; (b) index minimum transaction size is 20 t, so ~50–75 t/month is transactable but unremarkable; (c) container-scale orders forgo bulk-vessel freight economics, though **most of the volume penalty for a Botswana buyer is likely to show up in freight and in FCL utilisation, not in the mill's ex-works price.** This band is an inference, not a reported figure — do not present it to a supplier as a benchmark.
- **The bigger small-buyer risk is not the mill price but everything around it:** minimum reel-width and diameter runs, the mill's willingness to make a small reel spec at all, LC confirmation charges spread over a small value, part-container dead freight, and demurrage at Durban/Walvis Bay plus the road leg to Gaborone. A 5% mill premium is trivial next to a mis-specified reel that no local press can run.
- **Highest-leverage tactics for a buyer of this size, implied by the findings:**
  1. Buy **standard, index-defined spec** — 120 gsm, 18–22 BF recycled testliner is literally the grade Fastmarkets assesses in India, so it is the most liquid and most competitively quoted. Bespoke BF/gsm combinations invite a premium.
  2. Ask each mill for a **price ladder** (1, 5, 10, 25 containers/month) in writing — this converts an unknowable premium into a supplier-disclosed one.
  3. Quote **both an Indian mill route (FOB + CFR Durban/Walvis Bay) and a South African route (ex-works + DAP Gaborone)**; for a small volume the South African route's shorter, road-based, SACU-internal supply chain may beat a cheaper Indian mill price. Note the September 2026 SA tariff review could change this.
  4. Consider buying through a **regional distributor** where 600–900 t/yr is a large account (see §6 inference) rather than direct from a mill where it is a rounding error.
