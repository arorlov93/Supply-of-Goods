## A1. Ocean services Turkey → US East Coast / Gulf: carriers, frequency, transit times

### Takeaway
Turkey–US East Coast is a well-served direct lane out of Aliağa, Ambarlı/Istanbul, Gemlik/Izmit and Mersin, with Turkon Line running the flagship direct service to New York, Norfolk and Savannah at roughly 14–24 days port-to-port depending on load port. Direct calls at **Miami and Port Everglades are not confirmed in any source found**; South Florida is generally reached via Savannah/NY-NJ relay or transhipment, which is the single most important routing fact for a Florida-focused stone programme.

### Cited Findings
- Turkon Line's America & Mediterranean service offers direct sailings from **Mersin (MIP), Istanbul (Marport), Izmit (Evyap), Gemlik (Gemport) and Aliağa (TCEEGE)** to **New York (APM), Norfolk (NIT) and Savannah (GPA)** — [Turkon Line, America & Mediterranean Service](https://turkon.com/en/services/america-mediterranean-service)
- Turkon transit times: **Mersin → New York ~20 days, → Norfolk ~22 days, → Savannah ~24 days**; **Aliağa → New York ~14 days** (described by the carrier as the fastest direct Turkey–US connection), **→ Norfolk ~16 days, → Savannah ~18 days** — [Turkon Line](https://turkon.com/en/services/america-mediterranean-service)
- Generalist transit guidance for the lane: **25–35 days Istanbul → US East Coast** — [Cumbaco, Shipping from Turkey to USA 2026](https://cumbaco.com/insights/shipping-turkey-to-usa-2026/)
- Principal Turkish load ports characterised as: **Ambarlı (Istanbul)** highest volume, Marmara region; **Izmir/Aliağa** dominant for textiles/apparel; **Mersin** deepest water, serves southern Turkey (i.e. the Afyon/Burdur/Antalya marble belt's nearest deep-water outlet); **İskenderun** steel/industrial — [Cumbaco](https://cumbaco.com/insights/shipping-turkey-to-usa-2026/)
- US discharge port trade-offs as described by the same source: **Savannah** — cheapest dwell, fastest inland reach to the Southeast; **Norfolk** — faster clearance than NY/NJ; **NY/NJ** — largest market, longest dwell; **Houston** — Texas/Gulf. Routing via Savannah instead of Newark is claimed to save **USD 200–400/container** in dwell and chassis fees — [Cumbaco](https://cumbaco.com/insights/shipping-turkey-to-usa-2026/) *(ESTIMATE, vendor blog, May 2026)*

### Inferences
- Because Turkon's direct string terminates at Savannah and does not list a South Florida call, a Turkey → Miami/Port Everglades move will in practice be either (a) Savannah discharge + ~560 km truck/rail south, or (b) a transhipment routing on MSC/CMA CGM/ZIM via a Caribbean or NY/NJ hub, adding roughly a week. This should be priced as landed-to-Miami, not as ocean freight alone.
- The Aliağa 14-day figure matters commercially: it is competitive with intra-Med lead times and is a genuine selling point versus Chinese or Indian stone, whose East Coast transits typically run 35–50 days. (Comparison transit figures not sourced here — treat as directional.)

## A3. Container loading for stone — the weight constraint (CRITICAL)

### Takeaway
Stone is weight-limited, and the binding constraint is usually **not** the container's ISO rating but the **US 80,000 lb highway gross limit**, which caps cargo at roughly **19.5–20.5 t (43,000–45,000 lb)** for a routine non-permitted drayage move. One stone exporter states plainly that it loads **24 t into a 20ft for Europe but only 21 t for the USA** — that differential is the whole story. The realistic US-destination 20ft load is therefore about **19–21 t**, translating to roughly **370–420 m² of 2 cm** and **250–290 m² of 3 cm** marble.

### Cited Findings — container ratings and the CSC plate
- **20ft maximum payload is commonly cited at 28,200 kg**, but actual maximum payload "generally ranges from **21,700 to 28,200 kg** depending on the shipping line, route, and local regulations" — [ContainerOne, Shipping Container Weight Limits](https://containerone.net/blogs/news/shipping-container-weight-limits-payload-load-capacity-the-complete-guide); [FreightAmigo 20ft container guide](https://www.freightamigo.com/en/blog/logistics/20ft-container-dimensions-complete-guide-to-sizes-weights-and-capacities/)
- **The CSC Safety Approval Plate governs, not the generic spec.** IMO rule: a packed container must not exceed the maximum gross mass shown on its CSC Safety Approval Plate, normally fixed to the container door, which identifies the approved maximum operating gross mass **for that specific unit** — [ContainerOne](https://containerone.net/blogs/news/shipping-container-weight-limits-payload-load-capacity-the-complete-guide)
- Explicit caution that "actual tare and payload vary by manufacturer and container generation, so always cross-check the CSC plate on the specific unit before booking the cargo weight" — [ContainerOne](https://containerone.net/blogs/news/shipping-container-weight-limits-payload-load-capacity-the-complete-guide)
- **Typical max gross mass for standard 20ft and 40ft units is around 30,480 kg** — [ContainerOne](https://containerone.net/blogs/news/shipping-container-weight-limits-payload-load-capacity-the-complete-guide)
- For tile loading purposes, a trade source uses **20ft max payload 28,200 kg and 40ft max payload 28,800 kg** — i.e. **only 600 kg more payload in a box of double the volume** — [Riwick, Container Loading Tiles](https://riwick.com/container-loading-tiles/)

### Cited Findings — US road weight limits (the real cap)
- Under **23 U.S.C. §127** the maximum gross vehicle weight on the Interstate System is **80,000 lb** for tractor + chassis + container + cargo combined, distributed as **12,000 lb steer axle and 34,000 lb per tandem** — [Intek Logistics, Intermodal Container Weight Limits](https://www.inteklogistics.com/blog/intermodal-container-weight-limits-what-shippers-need-to-load-legally); [Oversize.io overweight container guide](https://oversize.io/regulations/overweight-shipping-container-guide)
- In practice this **restricts cargo to approximately 44,000–48,000 lb (≈20.0–21.8 t) for a 40ft**, with one source giving **44,500 lb (≈20.2 t)** as the maximum payload for a 40ft in road drayage — [Intek Logistics](https://www.inteklogistics.com/blog/intermodal-container-weight-limits-what-shippers-need-to-load-legally); [Nypul, Maximum Weight for a Drayage Container](https://nypul.com/what-is-the-maximum-weight-for-a-drayage-container/)
- These container figures "represent the maximum structural capacity of the containers themselves, and the actual allowable weight for drayage operations is often lower due to road regulations"; some states allow higher with **special overweight permits** — [Truck Packer, Overweight Container Trucking 2026](https://www.truckpacker.com/newsletter/overweight-container-trucking-us-road-weight-limits-shipping-containers)

### Cited Findings — what exporters actually load
- A stone exporter's own loading rule, and the clearest single data point in this file: **"For European destination, we always put 24 metric tons in the container. For USA destination we always put 21 metric tons in the 20ft container."** — [Rockstone, Container Capacity & Conversion](https://www.rockstone.biz/container-capacity-conversion/)

- Cost of mis-loading: a container loaded 15% below weight capacity raises per-unit ocean freight by ~25%; on a USD 4,000 freight bill that is **~USD 1,000 wasted per box** — [Riwick](https://riwick.com/container-loading-tiles/) — **ESTIMATE**

### Inferences (arithmetic, clearly labelled as derived — not sourced figures)
- **Density cross-check.** Marble ≈ 2.7 t/m³ → 2 cm ≈ **54 kg/m²**, 3 cm ≈ **81 kg/m²**. Travertine is lighter, ≈ 2.4–2.5 t/m³ → 2 cm ≈ **48–50 kg/m²**. (Densities are standard textbook values, not sourced in this research — verify against the quarry's own test data.)
- Applying 21 t (Rockstone's stated US load) to those densities: 2 cm → **~389 m²** before packing weight; 3 cm → **~259 m²**. Deducting A-frames/bundle timber (commonly 500–1,000 kg per container, **UNVERIFIED**) gives roughly **370–390 m² at 2 cm** and **245–260 m² at 3 cm**. This sits at or just below the low end of the Rockstone table and well below the Turkish Marble Company figures.
- Back-solving the higher quotes: **500 m² of 2 cm ≈ 27 t** and **330 m² of 3 cm ≈ 27 t**. Those are *plate-limited European loads*, not US-deliverable loads. **A Turkish supplier quoting "500 m² per container" is very likely quoting a 27 t box that cannot legally be drayed in Florida without an overweight permit.** This is exactly the misjudgement the brief anticipates, and it should be caught at the PO stage, not at the port.
- **Constraint cascade for a US-bound 20ft of stone** (the honest way to present this when a hard number is not available):
  1. ISO/CSC plate max gross mass on the *specific* unit — typically 30,480 kg, but **must be read off the plate**; in-service units plated lower exist.
  2. minus tare (~2,200–2,400 kg for a 20ft) → nominal payload ~28,200 kg.
  3. minus carrier/route payload restriction → 21,700–28,200 kg depending on line.
  4. **minus the US 80,000 lb highway cap** → ~19.5–20.5 t of cargo for a standard non-permitted move; **this is normally the binding constraint.**
  5. minus dunnage, A-frames, bundle timber, strapping → net saleable stone tonnage.
  6. ÷ kg per m² at the given thickness and density → m² per container.
- **The 40HC is the wrong box for slabs.** With only ~600 kg more payload than a 20ft and a higher freight cost, the 40HC only pays for thin material (1–1.2 cm) or mixed loads with light goods. For 2 cm and 3 cm slabs the 20ft is the economic unit.

## A4. Packing: A-frames, crates, bundles, ISPM 15, breakage and claims

### Takeaway
ISPM 15 is a hard, non-negotiable border requirement — non-compliant wooden packaging from Turkey will be refused entry, not fined and released. Slabs must ship upright in A-frames or bundles; horizontal stacking is the classic cause of loss. Credible industry breakage figures are essentially absent from public sources.

### Cited Findings — ISPM 15
- Importers "must ensure that all wood packaging material entering or transiting the United States is pest-free, debarked, heat-treated or fumigated, and marked with an ISPM 15 logo"; **shipments containing non-compliant WPM will not be allowed to enter the country** — [USDA APHIS, Import ISPM 15-Compliant Wood Packaging Material into the United States](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/plant-imports/wood-packaging-material/import)
- Heat treatment = core temperature **≥56 °C for ≥30 minutes**, or approved methyl bromide fumigation; the mark must carry the **IPPC logo, country code, treatment code (HT), and a unique producer/treatment-facility identifier** — [Green Worldwide, Complying with ISPM 15](https://www.greenworldwide.com/what-shippers-need-to-know-about-the-international-standard-for-phytosanitary-measures-ispm-15/); [Twin Oaks Crates, ISPM 15 Compliance](https://twinoakscrates.com/2025/07/23/ispm-15-compliance-7-key-requirements-explained/)
- Türkiye has a national regulation on heat treatment and marking of wood packaging materials notified to the IPPC, requiring the treatment report to record date, the authorised treating company/person, and evidence of ≥56 °C for ≥30 minutes — [IPPC, Republic of Türkiye — Regulation on Heat Treatment and Marking of Wood Packaging Materials (March 2025)](https://www.ippc.int/en/countries/republic-turkiye/reportingobligation/2025/03/regulation-on-heat-treatment-and-marking-of-wood-packaging-materials/)

### Cited Findings — packing and breakage
- **Slabs should always be kept upright in A-frame racks or transport frames; horizontal stacking should be avoided** as it increases breakage risk — [SNT Stone, Guide to Marble Slab Sizes, Thicknesses & Packaging](https://sntstone.com/complete-guide-to-marble-slab-sizes-thicknesses-and-packaging/)
- "Improper packaging is the top cause of marble slab breakage during long-distance sea and land transportation," producing edge chipping, surface scratches, breakage, delays and insurance claims — [CN Speed One, How To Pack Marble Slabs For Export](https://cnspeedone.com/news/how-to-pack-marble-slabs-for-export-marble-slab-packing-guide/)
- Named A-frame failure modes: slab breakage, frame instability, forklift handling problems, shipping damage, warehouse congestion and **increased customer claims**; A-frames are frequently not designed to carry the slab weight or to stop remaining slabs shifting when one is withdrawn — [Heavyweight Transport, Navigating Slab Bundle Drayage Challenges](https://heavyweighttransportinc.com/navigating-slab-bundle-drayage-challenges-best-practices-for-safe-efficient-stone-transport-warehousing-in-2025/)
- OSHA has issued a dedicated safety bulletin on the hazards of transporting, unloading, storing and handling granite, marble and stone slabs — relevant because a US buyer's EHS team may ask how your A-frames are rated and secured — [OSHA SHIB 08-12-2008](https://www.osha.gov/publications/shib081208)
- Turkish slab reinforcement practice for transport includes **resin treatment and high-tensile fibreglass netting applied to the reverse face** — [Turkish Marble Company](https://turkishmarblecompany.com/blog/standard-marble-slab-sizes-a-professional-sourcing-and-specification-guide)
- One supplier claims a breakage rate of **<1%**, but this is a single vendor's self-reported figure, not an industry benchmark — [CN Speed One](https://cnspeedone.com/news/best-stone-export-packaging-solutions-granite-marble/)

### Inferences
- ISPM 15 is the cheapest catastrophic risk to eliminate: the cost of compliant treated timber is trivial against a refused container. The supplier's crate/A-frame timber supplier should be audited once, on paper, before the first shipment, and the HT mark photographed at loading as part of the loading-photo pack.
- Because A-frames are named as a source of *warehouse* claims as well as transit claims, the buyer's receiving capability matters: if the Florida consignee cannot handle A-frames with the right forklift attachments, breakage will be attributed to the supplier regardless of where it occurred. Agreeing a **joint tally and photographic survey at container opening** is the single highest-value clause in the contract.

## A6. Warehousing and drayage in South Florida

### Takeaway
Drayage from PortMiami/Port Everglades to a Miami-Dade or Broward warehouse runs roughly **USD 350–650 per container base**, before chassis, fuel and accessorials. Pallet storage in Miami is the most expensive Florida market at roughly **USD 15–28/pallet/month**. No source gives slab-specific or A-frame-rack storage pricing.

### Cited Findings — drayage
- **USD 350–650 per container** for most local port moves from PortMiami or Port Everglades to Miami-Dade or Broward warehouses, before fuel, chassis and accessorials — [Go Freight, Container Drayage Rates in Miami 2026](https://go-freight.io/container-drayage-rates-miami-2026/) — **ESTIMATE**
- **USD 350–550 per move** for a standard 40ft to Doral, Medley or Hialeah in 2026 — [Go Drayage, How Much Does Container Drayage Cost in Miami (2026)](https://godrayage.io/how-much-does-container-drayage-cost-in-miami/) — **ESTIMATE**
- Base rates from **USD 450 (Port Everglades) / USD 500 (PortMiami)** for specified carrier yards — [Go Warehouse](https://gowarehouse.io/drayage-rates-miami/) — **ESTIMATE**
- Accessorials: **chassis USD 30–60/day** (a second figure gives chassis days at **USD 25–45 each**), plus fuel surcharge, wait time, demurrage and per diem; per diem on a late empty return is described as dwarfing the original linehaul — [Go Warehouse](https://gowarehouse.io/drayage-rates-miami/); [Go Freight](https://go-freight.io/container-drayage-rates-miami-2026/) — **ESTIMATE**
- A public rate calculator exists for Miami/Everglades drayage — [Miami Drayage calculator](https://miamidrayage.com/miami-everglades-drayage-calculator)

### Cited Findings — warehousing
- **Florida 3PLs charge USD 15–28 per pallet per month** for storage; **Miami is the most expensive Florida market, running 30–50% above Orlando and Jacksonville** — [Mako Freight, How Much Does a 3PL Warehouse Cost in Florida?](https://www.makofreight.com/2026/05/12/how-much-does-a-3pl-warehouse-cost-in-florida/) — **ESTIMATE**
- Broader US range **USD 8–25+ per pallet position per month** depending on facility quality; **floor/bulk storage USD 12–22 per pallet per month**; **handling in/out USD 5–15 per pallet each way** — [WarehousingCosts.com, Pallet Storage Costs 2026](https://warehousingcosts.com/guides/pallet-storage-costs) — **ESTIMATE**
- South Florida-specific pallet storage pricing models discussed at [Miami Drayage, Understanding Pallet Storage Costs in South Florida](https://miamidrayage.com/news/understanding-pallet-storage-costs-and-pricing-models-in-south-florida)
- General 2026 warehouse space rates of **USD 1.25–2.25/sq ft** — [Suaid Global, Warehouse Costs 2026](https://suaidglobal.com/insights/warehouse-costs-2026/) — **ESTIMATE**

### Inferences
- Pallet-based 3PL pricing maps badly onto slabs. A slab A-frame or bundle is not a pallet position: it is a heavy, tall, floor-loaded footprint requiring a clamp or boom forklift. Expect any 3PL quote for slabs to be bespoke and to come with handling surcharges — and expect most general-merchandise 3PLs in Doral/Medley to decline the cargo outright.
- The practical South Florida warehousing answer for slabs is likely a **stone-specific distributor's yard or a shared-slab warehouse**, not a conventional 3PL. That is a sourcing exercise, not a price-list lookup.

## B1. Technical standards and testing the US market expects

### Takeaway
For natural stone the governing specifications are **ASTM C503 (marble)** and **ASTM C568 (limestone)**, which pull in the physical test methods; for tile the governing document is **ANSI A137.1**, whose **DCOF ≥0.42** slip-resistance threshold is the one number that has been written into the building code and therefore behaves as mandatory. Testing is customarily at the supplier's expense as a condition of being specified.

### Cited Findings — natural stone
- **ASTM C503/C503M** is the Standard Specification for Marble Dimension Stone; **ASTM C568/C568M** the Standard Specification for Limestone Dimension Stone — [ASTM C503/C503M-15](https://store.astm.org/c0503_c0503m-15.html); [ANSI Webstore, ASTM C568](https://webstore.ansi.org/standards/astm/astmc56803)
- The Natural Stone Institute maintains the authoritative mapping of which ASTM standards apply to natural stone and how they are used in specification — [NSI, Which ASTM Standards Are Relevant To Natural Stone](https://www.naturalstoneinstitute.org/designprofessionals/astm/); [NSI, ASTM International Standards](https://www.naturalstoneinstitute.org/stoneprofessionals/astm/)
- Both C503 and C568 reference **ASTM C1353/C1353M**, the rotary-platform abraser test for abrasion resistance of dimension stone subjected to foot traffic; C1353 establishes an index of abrasion resistance by volume loss — [ASTM standards listing via GlobalSpec, C503](https://standards.globalspec.com/std/1148309/ASTM%20C503)
- **Caution on abrasion values**: the older **ASTM C241/C241M** produces Ha values directly comparable to the C503/C568 minimums, while **C1353 measures something different and its result should not be read as interchangeable with a C241 figure** — [Dynamic Stone Tools, Abrasion Resistance Ratings for Stone Floors](https://dynamicstonetools.com/blogs/news/abrasion-resistance-ratings-for-stone-floors-granite-and-marble). A Turkish mill certificate quoting one method against the other's threshold is a classic spec-rejection trigger.

### Cited Findings — tile and slip resistance
- **ANSI A137.1-2012** adopted a minimum **DCOF of 0.42** per the DCOF AcuTest for level interior spaces expected to be walked on when wet; **ANSI A137.1:2022** carries the same requirement — [TCNA, Coefficient of Friction and the DCOF AcuTest (technical bulletin)](https://tcnatile.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/DCOFBrochure_Aug2013_Comp.pdf); [TileLetter, DCOF rating requirements](https://www.tileletter.com/dcof-interior-and-exterior-tile/)
- The test is run on a **BOT-3000E digital tribometer** with a hard rubber slider simulating a dress-shoe heel, under **ANSI A326.3 / A137.1** — [Safety Direct America, ANSI A326.3 Floor Slip Resistance Testing](https://safetydirectamerica.com/floor-friction-testing/ansi-a137-1-slip-testing/)
- **A137.1 was incorporated into the 2012 International Building Code and has replaced the old ASTM C1028 SCOF test; building inspectors may require it** — [Safety Direct America, ANSI Tile Spec A137.1](https://safetydirectamerica.com/does-ansi-a137-1-protect-you-from-liability-for-accidents/)
- Daltile's published guidance treats DCOF as the operative slip metric and gives situation-specific minimum values above 0.42 for wetter applications — [Daltile, DCOF AcuTest](https://www.daltile.com/why-daltile/industry-standards/dcof-slip-resistance-testing-reading-test-results); [Safety Direct America](https://safetydirectamerica.com/dal-tile-recommends-situation-specific-minimum-dcof-values/)

### Inferences
- **ASTM C1028 is obsolete.** A supplier arriving with a C1028 SCOF certificate is signalling that its technical file is a decade out of date. Re-test to A326.3/A137.1 before approaching US buyers.
- Practically, the mandatory/customary split runs as follows: **DCOF ≥0.42 is effectively mandatory** for wet interior floor tile because it is code-referenced; **C503/C568 conformance is contractually mandatory** whenever an architect writes it into a spec section (which is the norm on commercial work) and merely customary on residential distribution business; **C1353/C241 abrasion, C373 absorption and freeze-thaw are customary** but become mandatory the moment the application is exterior, cold-climate or commercial floor.
- **Who pays**: no source found states this explicitly, but the structure of the market — the buyer specifies, the supplier certifies — means the supplier will be expected to fund testing at a US-recognised laboratory and to re-test per lot or per quarry block campaign. Budget for it as a cost of market entry rather than expecting to bill it to the first customer.

### Gaps
- **UNKNOWN / NOT SOURCED**: the specific numeric thresholds in C503 and C568 for compressive strength, flexural strength, absorption and density. (The Ha ≥10 abrasion minimum appears in search summaries for both C503 and C568 but I could not verify it against the standard text, which is paywalled — **treat as unverified**.)
- **UNKNOWN**: ASTM C373 (water absorption) detail and the applicable freeze-thaw method (ASTM C1026 for tile) — not retrieved.

## B2. Certification and compliance

### Takeaway
Two things a Turkish supplier will not expect: **CE marking counts for nothing in the US**, and **Miami-Dade HVHZ has its own Notice of Acceptance regime that overrides even statewide Florida approval** for exterior cladding — which is precisely the application a Florida stone programme would target.

### Cited Findings
- **Natural Stone Institute Accreditation** is described as the only independent third-party credentialing programme for natural stone fabricators and installers, verified through document review, a written exam and an on-site inspection, and covering ethics, customer satisfaction, business practices, **insurance coverage** and safety. Categories: Natural Stone Fabricator, Commercial A installer, Commercial B installer — [NSI, Accreditation](https://www.naturalstoneinstitute.org/programs/accreditation/accreditation/); [NSI, Become an Accredited Company](https://www.naturalstoneinstitute.org/programs/accreditation/become-an-accredited-company/)
- NSI publishes **qualified-labor specification language** that architects insert into specs, which is how accreditation converts into commercial access — [NSI, Qualified Labor Specification Language](https://www.naturalstoneinstitute.org/programs/accreditation/qualified-labor-specification-language/)
- **Miami-Dade HVHZ**: the Miami-Dade Board and Code Compliance Office approves products for use in the High Velocity Hurricane Zone as described in the Florida Building Code and issues a **Notice of Acceptance (NOA)**; approved products are subject to periodic **Quality Assurance reviews** — [Miami-Dade County, Product Approval](https://www.miamidade.gov/global/economy/board-and-code/product-approval.page)
- **"Every exterior cladding material installed in Miami must carry a current Miami-Dade NOA... A product that holds a Florida statewide product approval but lacks a Miami-Dade NOA cannot legally be installed inside the HVHZ."** — [Siding Quotes, Miami FL HVHZ Permits & NOA](https://www.sidingquotesnearme.com/siding/florida/miami) *(secondary source; consistent with the Miami-Dade page above and with Florida Building Code training material)*
- Worked precedent for stone cladding: the **Gridworx Ultra stone cladding system with Dekton** obtained Miami-Dade NOAs **24-0123.03 and 24-0123.04** after wind-load testing — evidence that the route exists for stone and is taken via the **cladding system**, not the stone slab alone — [Gridworx Walls, Miami-Dade wind load test](https://gridworxwalls.com/miami-dade-wind-load-test-blown-away-by-dekton-with-the-gridworx-ultra-stone-cladding-system/)
- Official training material on navigating Miami-Dade NOAs and Florida Product Approvals — [Florida Building Commission course PDF](http://floridabuilding.org/Upload/Courses_trp/588-0-MATERIAL-Advanced%20Navigating%20into%20Miami-Dade%20NOA's%20and%20Florida%20Product%20Approvals.pdf)
- **California Prop 65**: almost all ceramic/porcelain and natural stone products contain crystalline silica, which falls under Prop 65; cutting, grinding, sanding or polishing generates respirable silica dust, listed by California as a carcinogen. Prop 65 **does not ban sale** — it requires a warning on the product, packaging or literature — [Virginia Tile, Prop 65](https://virginiatile.com/prop-65/); [Tile Club, Prop 65 Warning for Tile](https://www.tileclub.com/pages/prop-65-warning-for-tile); [Ceramic Tile Design, Prop 65](https://ceramictiledesign.com/prop-65/)
- A US importer/distributor of natural stone publishes a **Safety Data Sheet for natural stone tile and slab** — the format a US buyer will expect from the supplier — [Marblex Corp SDS for Natural Stone Tile and Slab](https://marblexcorp.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/SAFETY-DATA-SHEET-for-Natural-Stone-WOrd-Version-TEST.pdf)

### Inferences
- **NSI accreditation is aimed at fabricators and installers, not at overseas quarriers/exporters.** A Turkish supplier cannot realistically obtain it and should not promise it; what it can do is supply the technical data that lets its *US customer* satisfy an NSI-referenced spec. Selling to NSI-accredited fabricators is the better play than trying to become one.
- **Prop 65 is a labelling and paperwork obligation, not a barrier**, and it is cheap to satisfy: a compliant warning on cartons/packaging and an SDS in the technical pack. Failing to provide these is a common and entirely avoidable reason a US distributor will not onboard a new overseas vendor.
- **The HVHZ NOA sits with the cladding system, not the stone.** For a Turkish stone supplier the realistic Florida exterior play is to supply into an already-NOA'd anchoring system (Gridworx-type) rather than to pursue an NOA in its own name. For interior flooring and countertops — the bulk of the market — HVHZ is irrelevant.

- **UNKNOWN**: whether TCNA operates a supplier "certification" as such. TCNA publishes the TCNA Handbook and administers ANSI A137.1 test methods; no supplier-certification scheme was confirmed in the sources retrieved.
- **UNKNOWN**: cost and duration of obtaining a Miami-Dade NOA for a stone cladding assembly.
- **UNKNOWN**: whether any Florida-specific requirement applies to interior floor/wall stone beyond the Florida Building Code's adoption of ANSI/IBC slip provisions.

## B3. Commercial terms US buyers typically offer a new overseas supplier

### Takeaway
The pattern is well established in general import practice — **LC or substantial deposit on the first orders, migrating to TT and then to open account 30/60/90 days once payment history exists** — but I found **no stone- or tile-specific data** on first-order sizes or trial-order norms.

### Cited Findings
- Letters of credit are "most often used in higher-risk situations or **new trade relationships**" and are "common for trial orders because it reduces risk for suppliers who don't yet know your payment history" — [Sellers Union, Letter of Credit Explained](https://www.sellersunionagent.com/letter-of-credit/)
- Common credit terms in international trade are **30, 60 or 90 days after shipment**; LC is "most suitable for large orders and new supplier relationships" — [JMS Decor, Supplier Payment Terms: T/T, L/C](https://jmsdecor.com/china-supplier-payment-terms/)
- **Open account terms are typically only offered once payment history is built** — [Sellers Union](https://www.sellersunionagent.com/letter-of-credit/)
- The LC carries "additional costs, strict documentation requirements, and longer processing times" — [Sellers Union](https://www.sellersunionagent.com/letter-of-credit/)
- US government primer on LC mechanics for exporters — [trade.gov, Letter of Credit](https://trade.gov/letter-credit); [Shipping Solutions, Methods of Payment in International Trade: Letters of Credit](https://shippingsolutionssoftware.com/blog/methods-of-payment-in-international-trade-letters-of-credit)
- Short-term trade finance products exist specifically for trial import orders — [Financely, Short-Term Trade Finance for Trial Import Orders](https://www.financely.io/shortterm-trade-finance-for-trial-import-orders)

### Inferences
- Note the direction of risk: in these sources the LC protects the **exporter** against an unknown buyer. A Turkish supplier approaching US buyers should expect the reverse pressure — US distributors with market power will push for **TT after delivery or open account**, and will treat an insistence on confirmed LC as friction. The workable compromise on a first container is typically a deposit plus balance against documents, with credit insurance on the receivable rather than an LC.
- A single container is the natural trial unit for this product, and it is small enough that LC issuance costs (bank fees, discrepancy fees) are disproportionate — which argues further for TT with a deposit on order one, and for the supplier to carry the risk via export credit insurance.

- The only adjacent sourced fact: tile thickness tolerance of **±0.5 mm can swing container cargo mass by hundreds of kilos after sample approval** — i.e. the sample-approval document must pin dimensional tolerance, not just appearance, or the logistics plan breaks — [Riwick](https://riwick.com/container-loading-tiles/)
- Natural stone slab reinforcement (resin, fibreglass mesh backing) is standard Turkish production practice and is relevant to consistency claims because resin treatment alters surface appearance batch to batch — [Turkish Marble Company](https://turkishmarblecompany.com/blog/standard-marble-slab-sizes-a-professional-sourcing-and-specification-guide)

### Inferences
- Because no public standard governs shade matching, this is always contractual. The defensible mechanism in this trade is **block/lot traceability**: quote and ship against a named quarry block or production lot, retain a physical control sample of each lot signed by both parties, and state explicitly in the contract that colour is guaranteed *within a lot* and only *approximately* across lots. Overpromising "same shade on re-order" against a natural material is a failure mode, not a selling point.

## B6. Why new overseas suppliers fail with US buyers in this category

### Takeaway
No source directly addresses supplier failure modes in this category. What can be assembled from the sourced material is a list of *mechanisms* that demonstrably cause rejection or loss, each traceable to a finding above. These are inferences, flagged as such.

### Cited Findings (the underlying mechanisms, each sourced)
- **Border refusal on wood packaging**: non-compliant WPM shipments "will not be allowed to enter the country" — [USDA APHIS](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/plant-imports/wood-packaging-material/import)
- **Wrong test method**: ASTM C1028 SCOF has been replaced by ANSI A137.1/A326.3 DCOF in the IBC — [Safety Direct America](https://safetydirectamerica.com/does-ansi-a137-1-protect-you-from-liability-for-accidents/); and C1353 abrasion results are not interchangeable with C241 Ha thresholds — [Dynamic Stone Tools](https://dynamicstonetools.com/blogs/news/abrasion-resistance-ratings-for-stone-floors-granite-and-marble)
- **Statewide approval is not HVHZ approval**: a Florida statewide product approval without a Miami-Dade NOA cannot legally be installed in the HVHZ — [Siding Quotes](https://www.sidingquotesnearme.com/siding/florida/miami); [Miami-Dade Product Approval](https://www.miamidade.gov/global/economy/board-and-code/product-approval.page)
- **Packaging is the top cause of loss**: "improper packaging is the top cause of marble slab breakage during long-distance sea and land transportation" — [CN Speed One](https://cnspeedone.com/news/how-to-pack-marble-slabs-for-export-marble-slab-packing-guide/)
- **Weight misjudgement is endemic**: "tiles kill payload before volume"; 15% under-loading raises per-unit freight ~25% — [Riwick](https://riwick.com/container-loading-tiles/); and US loads must be cut to ~21 t vs 24 t for Europe — [Rockstone](https://www.rockstone.biz/container-capacity-conversion/)
- **Missing Prop 65 labelling** on product, packaging or literature is a compliance defect in California-bound goods — [Virginia Tile](https://virginiatile.com/prop-65/)

### Inferences (failure modes, derived from the above — not directly sourced)
1. **Quoting European container loads to a US buyer.** Promising 500 m² of 2 cm per 20ft produces a ~27 t box that cannot be drayed in Florida under the 80,000 lb cap. The result is a split container, an overweight permit, or a refused move — and the cost lands on the supplier's credibility in week one.
2. **ISPM 15 failure on A-frames and crates.** Entirely preventable, catastrophic when it happens, and it happens to first-time exporters who treat dunnage as scrap timber.
3. **An out-of-date technical file.** C1028 certificates, CE marks, and abrasion figures quoted against the wrong method read to a US specifier as a supplier who has never sold into this market.
4. **No SDS, no Prop 65 warning, no ASTM-referenced test report.** US distributors have vendor-onboarding checklists; a missing SDS stops onboarding before price is ever discussed.
5. **Overpromising shade consistency on re-order**, then failing to deliver it on order three — the classic natural-stone relationship-killer, because the claim arrives after the buyer has already specified the material on a project.
6. **No US-side receiving capability.** Slabs arriving on A-frames at a general 3PL that cannot handle them generates damage that will be charged back to the supplier regardless of fault.
7. **Rate and lead-time volatility absorbed as a fixed price.** With 40ft rates quoted in a USD 4,000–7,000 band and transits of 25–35 days plus a South Florida leg, a supplier quoting DDP at a fixed price for a season is underwriting freight risk it cannot hedge.

The seven failure modes above are reasoned from the sourced mechanisms, not reported by a source, and should be presented in the report as analysis rather than as findings.
